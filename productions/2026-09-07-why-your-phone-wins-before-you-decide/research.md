# Research — 2026-09-07

## Primary peer-reviewed evidence
Oulasvirta, Rattenbury, Ma & Raita (2012), Habits make smartphone use more pervasive, Personal and Ubiquitous Computing 16, 105–114, DOI 10.1007/s00779-011-0412-2.
Read the University of Helsinki research record/abstract. Three studies describe brief repetitive checking; quickly accessible information can reinforce checking. The paper reports annoyance rather than treating all habitual use as addiction. [3](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/habits-make-smartphone-use-more-pervasive/)

## Accessible corroboration and trigger evidence
University of Washington news report, 29 April 2019, Patterns of compulsive smartphone use suggest how to kick the habit. Read the full main article. Interviews with 39 Seattle-area smartphone users aged 14–64 identified unoccupied moments, tedious tasks, awkward situations and anticipated messages as reported triggers. Interview findings are not a randomized causal test or a universal claim. [1](https://www.washington.edu/news/2019/04/29/patterns-of-compulsive-smartphone-use/)

## Script claim map
- "checking habit": direct plain-language definition from Oulasvirta et al. [3](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/habits-make-smartphone-use-more-pervasive/)
- "quiet moment can become the cue": qualified illustration of interview-reported unoccupied-moment triggers. [1](https://www.washington.edu/news/2019/04/29/patterns-of-compulsive-smartphone-use/)
- "new information ... can reinforce another check": study-based mechanism, no dopamine/brain-hijacking claim. [3](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/habits-make-smartphone-use-more-pervasive/)
- Willpower line is a qualified reframe of habitual checking, not a claim that agency disappears.
- Phone out of sight/reach for a work block is a modest practical experiment in changing the setup, not claimed to be the tested intervention in either study, not a cure or a cognitive-capacity guarantee.
- Do not use the controversial blanket "mere presence drains your brain" claim, addiction diagnosis, invented statistics, or treatment recommendations.

## Distinct editorial angle
This Short covers unplanned checking during pauses, not notification interruption, variable-reward addiction, or generic screen-time panic. Later calendar items on notifications/learning/habits must use distinct evidence and situations instead of repeating this script.
