Why can a two-minute task sit on your list for three days? Making it small doesn't always make it clear. Take “work on the report.” Sounds reasonable. But what do your hands actually do first? Open a file? Find a number? Write a sentence? Meanwhile, your desk has somehow become the cleanest desk in Europe. Here's the useful distinction: a goal tells you what you want. A concrete action tells you what to do. In small experiments, students prompted to think concretely tended to return a questionnaire sooner than students prompted to think abstractly. The researchers measured when answers arrived, not a magical burst of motivation. And that doesn't prove that rewriting a to-do list cures procrastination. But it suggests a practical experiment. Keep the goal. Change the first instruction. Instead of “work on the report,” try “open the draft and write one rough sentence about the main result.” You can picture the file, the movement, and what counts as done. The sentence is allowed to be bad. It's a first sentence, not a marriage proposal. Then choose the next useful action, rather than spending the session perfecting your plan. If the real problem is exhaustion, anxiety, or an impossible workload, smaller wording won't solve that. But when the first move is fuzzy, don't just ask, “How can I make this easier?” Ask, “What exactly would starting look like?”
