# Research before script: Name It To Tame It — carefully

Three angles: (1)2007fMRI amygdala/RVLPFC brain story: eye-catching but risks the exaggerated neuro-claims the channel forbids; used only as background. (2) Behaviour-level finding that naming fear during real exposure lowered later physiological arousal while self-reported fear did not differ, chosen because the honest twist (body calms, feelings still feel the same) is the non-obvious payoff. (3) Journaling/expressive writing generally: too broad, overlaps future planned entries. Curiosity/evidence/visual/payoff: angle1=2/1/1/1, angle2=2/2/2/2, angle3=1/1/1/1.

Semantic overlap check against all90 calendar titles and produced videos: distinct from planning triggers, restart notes, retrieval, familiarity, spotlight, liking gap, reference dependence. Later planned entries on suppression ("pushing a thought away") and replaying conversations must stay separate: this one is about labeling emotion during a feared situation.

Primary: Kircanski, Lieberman & Craske (2012), Psychological Science, DOI10.1177/0956797612443830. Abstract read directly at https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797612443830 (full text paywalled; no full-methods claim). Spider-fearful participants repeatedly exposed to a live spider, between-subjects: affect labeling vs reappraisal vs distraction vs exposure alone. At one-week posttest with a DIFFERENT spider in another context the affect-labeling group showed reduced skin conductance response relative to other groups and MARGINALLY greater approach than distraction, but did NOT differ in self-reported fear. More anxiety/fear words used, greater reductions.

Corroboration, independent-ish but overlapping authors: Niles, Craske, Lieberman & Hur (2015), Behaviour Research and Therapy68, DOI10.1016/j.brat.2015.03.004, open version https://escholarship.org/content/qt91f398qp/qt91f398qp.pdf .102participants with public speaking anxiety randomized to exposure with or without affect labeling; greater reduction in physiological activation, especially for those using more labels; again NO self-report effect. Shared senior authors, so treat as replication-in-lab-line, not independent confirmation.

Mechanism background only: Lieberman et al.(2007), Psychological Science18:421–428, N=30fMRI, https://teams.semel.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/publications/May%202007%20-%20Putting%20Feelings%20Into%20Words.pdf chunks0,2 read. Affect labeling produced less amygdala activity than gender labeling (d=0.79) and increased RVLPFC. Small imaging sample, correlational mediation. Do NOT say naming a feeling switches off the amygdala, rewires the brain or replaces therapy.

Allowed claims: labeling emotion during exposure reduced later physiological arousal versus other strategies; it did not make people report feeling less afraid; more specific words tracked bigger reductions. Explicitly not: a cure for anxiety, a promise of feeling better, evidence about disorders in general, or a claim about everyday sadness. Viewer payoff: use plain concrete words for what you feel; expect your body to settle before your feelings agree.
