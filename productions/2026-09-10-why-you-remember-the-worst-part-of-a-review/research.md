## Primary evidence

**Redelmeier & Kahneman 1996** — reported in detail by Kahneman, "Experienced
Utility and Objective Happiness: A Moment-Based Approach", chapter 37 of *Choices,
Values and Frames* (2000), pp. 673–692.
https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/faculty/keith.chen/negot.%20papers/Kahneman_ExperiencedUtility00.pdf

- Design: patients undergoing colonoscopy reported current pain **every 60 seconds**
  on a 0–10 scale (10 = "intolerable pain"). Afterwards they gave a global
  retrospective evaluation of the whole procedure.
- **Duration neglect**: procedures ranged **4 to 69 minutes**, yet the correlation
  between duration and the patient's later evaluation was **.03**. Duration also had
  no effect on the hypothetical choice between a repeat colonoscopy and a barium
  enema.
- **Peak/End rule**: the retrospective evaluation was predicted at **r = .67** from
  the average of (a) the most intense pain reported and (b) the mean pain over the
  **last three minutes**.
- Patients A and B: B objectively had the worse procedure (more total pain), but A
  remembered it as worse, because A's ending was worse.

**Katz, Redelmeier & Kahneman 1997 / Redelmeier, Katz & Kahneman 2003** — clinical
randomized trial, **682 patients**. Half were randomly assigned to have the scope
left in place, unmoved, for about an extra minute after the examination ended —
distinctly uncomfortable but much less painful than what preceded it. Patients were
not told. Retrospective evaluations were **significantly more favourable** in the
extended group, despite strictly more total discomfort. Reported downstream: higher
rate of returning for repeat screening.

**Schreiber & Kahneman 2000** — same violation of dominance with sounds: 10 s at
78 db versus the same 10 s plus 4 s at 66 db. Most participants chose to repeat the
*longer* sound, even though 4 seconds of silence would obviously beat 4 seconds of
noise.

**Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier 1993**, *Psychological Science*,
"When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End" — the original
cold-pressor study (hand in 14 °C water for 60 s, versus the same 60 s plus 30 s
while the water warms slightly). A majority chose to repeat the longer trial.
Accessed via secondary description only; used as background, not as a source of
quoted numbers.

## Counter-evidence and boundaries

- The rule describes memory of **a bounded episode** with a clear start and end. It
  is not a licence to make long experiences worse.
- Repetition defeats it: a daily-bad experience creates a new bad memory each day;
  duration neglect does not protect against accumulated grudges.
- Some studies find participants do **not** neglect duration for negative
  experiences, so this is a robust-but-bounded finding, not a universal law.
- Asymmetry: the effect is strongest for aversive episodes. For pleasant ones,
  duration neglect appears softer.
- Ethics: the 682-patient trial did not inform patients of the manipulation, which
  has been debated in the bioethics literature. The video's practical advice is
  therefore aimed at how you *read your own* feedback and how you *end* things
  honestly — not at covertly manipulating others.

## Practical advice vs what was actually tested

- **Tested:** changing the ending of an aversive medical procedure changed its
  remembered unpleasantness and return behaviour.
- **Not tested:** performance reviews, break-ups, work days. Applying it to a review
  is an extrapolation, and the script keeps it as an "if that is how memory works,
  then" step rather than a claimed result.
- Practical step that follows honestly: when a review ends on the criticism, the
  memory will be built from that criticism, so **re-read the whole record before you
  decide what the review meant** — and when you run the meeting, do not let the
  worst sentence also be the last one.

## Viewer promise

One sentence: *Your memory of a long experience is built almost entirely from its
worst moment and its final minutes — which is why a mostly-good review can feel
mostly bad.*
