# Evidence and boundaries — 2026-09-07

Primary: Koudenburg, Postmes & Gordijn (2011), Disrupting the flow: How brief silences in group conversations affect social needs. JESP 47(2), 512–515. DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2010.12.006. Read the abstract, theory, methods, and the detailed results/manipulation checks in PDF chunk 1. The scenario and video paradigms asked participants to imagine taking part. Interrupted flow was associated with reduced validation/belonging and greater rejection feelings, not actual rejection. The interruption followed a mildly controversial statement, an important limit to broad everyday generalization. [Primary paper](https://www.rug.nl/staff/n.koudenburg/koudenburgetal.2011.pdf)

Accessible corroboration: the authors' public explanation, When conversations flow (2012), In-Mind. Describes how turn-taking/flow can act as a cue to connection and agreement. This is an accessible account by the same authors, not an independent replication. [Authors’ explanation](https://www.in-mind.org/article/when-conversations-flow)

Script uses "can", "may", "might" and explicitly describes imagined group participation. We do NOT state a universal four-second threshold, claim the study demonstrated subjective time slowing, or diagnose social anxiety. Original calendar title about silence feeling long is narrowed to **Why a Tiny Silence Can Feel So Awkward** because the actual evidence concerns social feelings, not elapsed-time perception. Ordinary thinking/sipping/searching for a word are plausible alternatives, not measured prevalence claims. Giving room and asking a follow-up are modest conversational suggestions, not a treatment or the tested intervention.

Distinctness: this video concerns interrupted conversational coordination, not the prior spotlight-effect mechanism or phone-checking habit. Visuals are fictional AI-generated cafe scenes, not a reconstruction of the study participants.

Important detail from the full methods/results: Study 2 DID ask participants to estimate the response delay as a manipulation check. Estimated delay did not differ significantly between flow and disrupted-flow conditions. Thus it offers no demonstrated time-dilation effect; our title/script correctly focus on inferred social acceptance instead.
